Abstract

This thesis argues that the fragmentation of modernist narrative registers a crisis of imperial authority rather than a purely aesthetic development. Drawing on manuscript evidence and contemporary reviews, it shows that formal difficulty concentrates precisely where imperial content appears.

1. Introduction

It is obvious that every modernist writer was responding to imperial decline. Clearly, fragmentation always signals political anxiety, and this proves that aesthetic form is entirely determined by historical circumstance. No one can deny that the connection is undeniable.

The critical literature has approached this question from several directions. Jameson (1981) reads formal discontinuity as a symptom of an unrepresentable totality, and Said (1993, p. 190) traces the same pressure through the imperial archive. Their accounts differ in emphasis but converge on the claim that modernist difficulty is historically produced rather than merely stylistic.
